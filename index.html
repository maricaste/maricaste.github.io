ADMITTED?

Who gets in?

The 2026 World Cup was played in three countries, but the border most fans had to cross belonged to one. We read the arrivals data alongside ticket prices and the stories of the people turned away.

What this project asks

Arrivals numbers tell us that people entered the United States, not why they came or what it took to get here. This project pairs monthly visitor counts from the National Travel and Tourism Office (NTTO) with ticket pricing and documented cases of players, teams, and fans who were refused entry or delayed, to ask who the tournament was actually open to.

UCLA Digital Humanities 101, Team C5.

Research questions

Arrivals and participation

  1. How did the 2026 FIFA World Cup influence international travel to the United States, and what can the changes reveal about global interest in the tournament?
  2. Did certain participating countries experience a larger increase in U.S. visitor arrivals than others, and what factors contributed to these differences?
  3. How did the success of national teams influence travel patterns among their supporters compared with supporters of teams eliminated earlier?
  4. Did World Cup related travel patterns differ between continents, and what could explain regional differences?
  5. To what extent did the World Cup attract visitors from countries that historically sent relatively few travelers to the U.S.?

Access and restrictions

  1. How did U.S. travel restrictions affect the ability of fans from certain participating countries to attend?
  2. How did arrivals from travel-restricted World Cup countries compare with arrivals from participating countries not subject to the same restrictions?
  3. How did the experiences of fans from travel-restricted countries compare with those from countries allowed visa-free travel?
  4. How might visa requirements, travel costs, and immigration policies have contributed to unequal opportunities for international fans?
  5. What might changes in arrivals from travel-restricted countries between 2025 and 2026 reveal about the relationship between international events and U.S. immigration policies?

Stories and the data

  1. What do news articles or personal stories say about the experiences and challenges travelers faced entering the U.S. for the World Cup?
  2. How do those stories compare with the patterns in the arrivals data?

What the map can’t tell us

I-94 counts record entry, not purpose or destination. A rise in June or July does not by itself mean those travelers came for the tournament, and monthly totals can’t separate match days from the weeks between them.

Data critique

What the dataset includes

Temporary international visitor arrivals to the U.S. from 2000 to 2026, drawn from I-94 admission records and organized by country of residence and month, with air-only categories for Mexico and Canada and a separate sheet of year-over-year percentage change.

What it cannot reveal

Why people traveled, where they went in the U.S., and how many came for the tournament. It also leaves out travel to the other two host countries and some Mexican visitors within the frontier zone.

Origins and ontology

The NTTO, part of the Department of Commerce’s International Trade Administration, compiled the data from CBP I-94 records, Statistics Canada’s International Travel Survey, and Mexican agencies (INEGI and Banco de México). Funding for this specific dataset is not publicly disclosed. Sorting travelers by country of residence and month gives a U.S. government view of travel, and reduces fans to counts.
